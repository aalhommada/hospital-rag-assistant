# Preparing for an MRI scan

An MRI scanner uses a very strong magnet and radio waves to take detailed pictures of the inside of your body. It does not use X-rays, and the scan itself is painless.

## Eating and drinking

For most MRI scans you may eat and drink normally right up to your appointment. Take your usual medicines at your usual times unless a doctor has told you otherwise.

If your appointment letter says you are having an MRI of the abdomen, liver, pancreas, or small bowel, you must not eat for six hours before the scan. You may drink water during those six hours. If you are diabetic, telephone the Radiology department on 020 7946 0121 before the day of your scan so your appointment can be moved to the morning.

## What to bring

Bring your appointment letter, a list of every medicine you take including doses, your reading glasses if you need them to fill in forms, and the name of your GP practice. If you have had a scan at another hospital in the last two years, bring the report or the disc if you have one.

## What not to bring

Leave jewellery, watches, and hair clips at home. The hospital cannot accept responsibility for valuables. You will be asked to remove anything metal, including underwired bras, hearing aids, and dentures with metal clasps.

## Safety questions you will be asked

Because of the magnet, the radiographer will ask you a set of safety questions before you enter the scanning room. Tell the radiology team in advance, not on the day, if any of the following apply to you.

You have a pacemaker, an implanted defibrillator, a cochlear implant, an insulin pump, a nerve stimulator, or any other implanted electronic device. You have a metal fragment in your eye or anywhere else from an injury or from working with metal. You have had brain, heart, or eye surgery. You are, or think you might be, pregnant. You weigh more than 200 kg, which is the safe limit for our scanner table.

## How long it takes

Most scans take between fifteen and forty-five minutes. Allow up to two hours in the department in total, because there is paperwork before the scan and you may have to wait if an emergency case arrives.

## Contrast injections

Some scans need a contrast agent, a dye given through a small needle in your arm, so that blood vessels and certain tissues show up more clearly. If you need one, it will usually be mentioned in your appointment letter, but the decision is sometimes made on the day.

If you have kidney disease or are on dialysis, tell the radiographer before the injection. You may need a blood test first.

## If you are claustrophobic

The scanner is an open-ended tube and it is noisy. If small spaces worry you, telephone Radiology at least one week before your appointment. We can arrange a longer appointment slot, allow someone to stay in the room with you, or ask your GP about a mild sedative. If you take a sedative, you must not drive afterwards and someone must take you home.

## Getting your results

Results are sent to the doctor who requested the scan, usually within ten working days. Urgent results are sent within twenty-four hours. Radiology cannot give you results directly, and the radiographer who performs the scan is not able to interpret it for you. Contact the doctor who referred you.

## Cancelling or changing your appointment

Telephone 020 7946 0121 as soon as you can, and at least two working days before your appointment, so the slot can be offered to someone else.
